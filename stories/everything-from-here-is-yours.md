# Everything From Here Is Yours

*A short story about StoreFlow*

---

The panic came, as usual, at 2:14 in the morning.

Kelechi knew the time because she was looking at her phone. The phone had a crack running corner to corner like a dry riverbed, and behind the crack was the code she had been writing for four months. Her chest got tight. Her hands went cold. The ceiling fan had stopped when NEPA took the light, and the room in Surulere was so quiet she could hear her own heart. It was going too fast, as if it were trying to leave.

*You are twenty-six,* the voice in her head said. *NYSC finished two years ago. No job. No money. Still in your aunty's house. And you are sitting here writing an app nobody asked for.*

She put the phone face-down on the mattress and did what the clinic nurse at LASUTH had taught her. Breathe in for four. Hold for four. Out for six. Name five things you can see.

The phone's small glow under the edge of the pillow.
Aunty Ngozi's wrapper hanging on the door.
A bag of detergent, Ariel, the big one.
Her laptop charger, which was useless without light.
A pile of laundry tickets on the floor, written in biro, half of them wrong.

The tickets were why she had started.

---

Aunty Ngozi ran *Sparkle Wash* from the front of the house. It was two washing machines, one generator, three boys who ironed, and a long wooden counter where customers dropped bundles of shirts, agbada, bedsheets and school uniforms. Everything lived in a school exercise book, and the exercise book was always lying.

"Kelechi, this man says he brought eleven shirts," Aunty would shout. "The book says nine. Who wrote nine?"

Nobody had written nine. Somebody had written *11* and a drop of water had turned it into a *9*.

Then there was money. Mrs. Adebayo from across the road owed ₦14,500 over three months and swore she had paid. Rent was due in six weeks. Diesel had gone up again. Every evening Aunty Ngozi sat with the exercise book and a calculator, and every evening she ended up with a different number and the same headache.

Kelechi understood that headache very well. It was not really about the money. It came from *not knowing*, from a fog where anything could be wrong and you couldn't see what. Her panic attacks came from the same fog.

So at night she wrote code that cleared it.

---

She called it **StoreFlow**.

The first version had just a counter. You typed what you sold and the price, and it added the numbers up properly, every time, without a calculator.

Then she made it work without data, because in Lagos the network drops like a bad habit. When a sale was saved offline, the phone kept it. When the network came back, a small banner said *"Back online — syncing your sales…"* and everything went up to the cloud quietly. Nothing was lost.

Then she added the laundry side, because that was the business in front of her. A new bundle got a code. It got the customer's name and phone number, the number of pieces, and a price from a price list Aunty set once and never had to remember again. When a bundle was ready, one tap opened WhatsApp with the message already written:

> *Good news — your laundry is ready for pickup from Sparkle Wash. Please bring or mention your laundry code when collecting.*

The first time Aunty Ngozi sent one, she read it out loud twice, very slowly, like a certificate.

"It is talking like a big company," she said. "Like Shoprite."

"It's talking like *you*," Kelechi said. "You just don't have to type it."

---

The hardest part wasn't the code. It was believing the code was worth anything.

Some weeks Kelechi couldn't open the laptop at all. The fog would roll in and every idea would look stupid. *Who will pay for this? Nigerians don't pay for apps. You are wasting light. You are wasting your life.*

On those days she did one small thing. Fix one bug. Change one button. Write one line of the setup guide. Then she closed the laptop and went to help fold towels, because folding towels was something her hands could finish.

She noticed something. Doing a small finished thing quieted the voice more than any amount of worrying did. So she built that into the app as well. When you record your very first sale, the screen celebrates. A gold ribbon comes down, and a little mascot in a shirt pumps its fist: *"Ka-ching! A new sale recorded!"*

It was silly and she knew it. But for a shop owner who had been fighting with an exercise book for ten years, and for a girl who had been fighting with her own chest for two, silly was good. Silly meant *you did something today, and it counted.*

---

The real test came on a Saturday in March, when Sparkle Wash had its busiest morning of the year. The Inter-house sports were on Monday, so every mother in the street needed white uniforms.

The network died at 9 a.m. NEPA followed at 9:20. The generator coughed twice and then, mercifully, caught.

Before, a morning like that would have cost Aunty Ngozi money she'd never find: bundles with no tickets, customers who "already paid", ironing boys guessing at prices. Kelechi stood behind the counter holding her breath, waiting for the fog.

It didn't come.

The phone kept taking bundles. *Twelve pieces, white uniforms, ₦3,600, ready Sunday.* When a customer said she would pay on pickup, Kelechi marked it as pending, so the amount sat in its own list instead of in somebody's memory. When the network came back at 1 p.m. the banner blinked, *Back online — syncing your sales…*, and forty-one bundles went up to the cloud in about the time it takes to sneeze.

That evening Aunty Ngozi did not open the exercise book. She sat on the plastic chair in front of the shop with a bottle of Maltina and scrolled through the day, bundle by bundle, naira by naira, with the same face Kelechi remembered from church.

"Mrs. Adebayo," Aunty said suddenly. "Look. She really did pay. Last month, fourteen thousand. It's here." She laughed. "I've been vexing that woman for nothing."

---

Near the end of the month, something happened that Kelechi hadn't really believed would happen.

She had set up StoreFlow to watch the month's costs: rent put aside, the boys' salaries, diesel, detergent. As sales came in it compared them against those costs. On the 27th, around four in the afternoon, a customer paid ₦2,400 for two duvets, and the screen changed.

> **Rent, salaries and the washing are covered.**
> **Everything from here is yours.**

Aunty Ngozi read it and sat down.

Kelechi thought she might cry. Then she realised her aunty was the one crying, quietly, the way hardworking women cry, pretending it was sweat.

"Ten years," Aunty said. "Ten years I've been doing this business and I didn't know which day I started making money for myself. I thought it was all going to the landlord."

"Now you know," Kelechi said.

"Now I know."

---

That night the panic came again, because it always did. Kelechi had learned that getting better wasn't a door you walk through once. It was more like a road you keep walking.

But this time, while she breathed in for four, held for four and let it out for six, she named different things.

The phone, crack and all, with forty-one bundles safely inside it.
Aunty's calculator, lying unused on the counter.
The WhatsApp message from Mama Tunde, who ran the provisions shop two streets away: *"Your aunty said you have an app. How much? I want it for my shop."*
Her own hands, warm now.
Tomorrow.

She typed back:

> *₦3,000 a month. First month free. I'll come and set it up myself.*

Mama Tunde replied with three thumbs up and a praying-hands emoji.

It was the first money anyone had ever offered Kelechi for something she had made from nothing. Three thousand naira wouldn't pay rent. But it was a start: one customer. The fog had never been able to argue with a real number.

She put the phone under the pillow, face up this time, and slept.

---

*The End.*
